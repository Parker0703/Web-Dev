This man survived an iron rod through his brain… but the people who knew him said he died that day.
Give me 60 seconds, and I'll show you what that rod took from him.
Vermont, 1848. Phineas Gage is a railroad foreman. Careful. Polite. The guy everyone trusts.
He's packing blasting powder into a rock with a 13-pound iron rod.
One spark.
The rod rockets up through his left cheek, behind his eye… and out the top of his skull.
Minutes later? He's talking. He rides into town sitting up in an ox cart. When the doctor arrives, Gage reportedly tells him, "Here's business enough for you."
He survives. But something's off.
The polite foreman is gone. Now he's rude, impatient, and swearing nonstop. His friends say he's "no longer Gage."
The rod had torn through his frontal lobe, the part of your brain that keeps you in check.
But here's the twist: years later, he's driving stagecoaches in Chile. Scientists think his brain slowly learned to adapt.
Today, his skull and the rod sit side by side at Harvard.
One accident… that changed how we understand the brain.
