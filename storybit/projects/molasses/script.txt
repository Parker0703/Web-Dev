A 25-foot wave just swallowed a city street… and it was made of syrup.
Boston. January 15th, 1919.
It was a strangely warm winter day in the North End.
Kids were playing, workers were eating lunch… right next to a giant steel tank.
Inside it? Over two million gallons of molasses.
Then, just after noon, people heard a sound like machine-gun fire.
That was the tank's rivets… shooting off, one by one.
The tank split wide open.
And a brown wave, 25 feet tall, rushed out at 35 miles per hour.
It smashed buildings, bent the steel girders of an elevated railway, and swept people and horses away.
But here's the twist: the wave wasn't the worst part.
As the cold January air hit it, the molasses got thicker and thicker… like wet cement.
People who survived the wave got stuck. The more they struggled, the deeper they sank.
Rescuers waded in up to their waists. Even horses couldn't pull free.
21 people died. Around 150 were injured.
So how does a tank just… explode?
It had leaked since the day it was built. The company's fix? Paint it brown, so the leaks wouldn't show.
It was never even properly tested before they filled it.
The company was held responsible, and the disaster helped bring in stricter building rules, so engineers had to sign off on projects like this.
And some Bostonians say that on hot summer days… the North End still smells sweet.
